# Airlines Diverge as Fuel Costs Spike and IATA Slashes Its 2026 Profit Outlook

**Why this is trending:** Oil prices near a 12-month high, driven by escalating Middle East tensions, pushed IATA to cut its 2026 global airline profit forecast to $23 billion — nearly half of 2025's $45 billion and well below its own earlier $41 billion projection — even as travel demand itself remains resilient. The same shock is hitting individual US carriers very differently: Delta has rallied over 20% year-to-date on premium-travel and loyalty-program strength, while United and American have faced steeper pressure from fuel-cost exposure.

**Linked companies:** Delta Air Lines (DAL), United Airlines (UAL), American Airlines (AAL)

## Bull case

Delta's loyalty and co-brand credit-card revenue provides a cash-flow cushion largely independent of flight volume, and premium-cabin and international demand has stayed resilient even as fuel costs climbed, letting Delta pass costs through via pricing power rather than absorb them. United's heavier exposure to international and corporate travel could make it a high-conviction contrarian play if fuel prices stabilize later in 2026, and the carrier is described as broadly under-owned by institutional funds despite an intact long-term growth thesis. Industry-wide, passenger demand and load factors remain historically strong — a record 84% load factor is still expected industry-wide — suggesting the current pressure is a margin story, not a demand story.

## Bear case

IATA's own forecast shows the industry's return on invested capital falling to 4.3% in 2026, below its roughly 8.5% cost of capital, meaning the sector overall may be destroying value on a capital basis even while revenue stays strong. American Airlines and smaller, weaker-balance-sheet carriers are explicitly flagged as carrying outsized fuel and financial risk with less ability to pass costs through, and competitive pressure plus softening consumer sentiment could limit every carrier's ability to raise fares enough to offset the fuel spike. The entire divergence-based bull case depends on Middle East tensions and oil prices moderating on a timeline no carrier controls.

## Risks

- **Geopolitical/fuel-price risk:** the sector's near-term earnings are directly exposed to Middle East tensions and oil prices, factors entirely outside any airline's control.
- **Capital-efficiency risk:** IATA's own math shows industry-wide returns on invested capital below the cost of capital in 2026, a structural concern beyond any single quarter's fuel spike.
- **Balance-sheet dispersion risk:** this basket intentionally spans carriers with very different risk profiles; a continued fuel spike could widen, not narrow, the performance gap between them.
- **Demand-elasticity risk:** softening consumer sentiment could limit carriers' ability to pass fuel costs through via fares, compressing margins further if fuel prices stay elevated.
